# Research Playbook — sources by contest type

How to find reliable, nonpartisan information for each kind of contest. Triangulate at least two independent sources before treating a claim as established. Prefer primary and nonpartisan sources over op-eds and campaign material.

## Tiered source quality

1. **Official / government** — county & state election offices (sample ballots, official results), legislative records, county-counsel ballot analyses, FEC. Authoritative for *what happened* and *what's on the ballot*.
2. **Nonpartisan civic infrastructure** — VOTE411 (League of Women Voters; candidate answers in their own words), Ballotpedia (encyclopedic profiles, records, sample ballots), Vote Smart (Political Courage Test, ratings, public statements).
3. **Money & influence trackers** — OpenSecrets (federal + some state), FollowTheMoney.org (50-state contributions, independent spending, ballot-measure money).
4. **Domain raters** — bar associations (judicial), interest-group scorecards (issue-specific records), reputable local newsrooms and their voter guides.
5. **Campaign material** — candidate sites, forums, debates. Useful for *stated* positions and plan specificity; never the sole source for a *record* claim.

**Red flags:** anonymous "voter guide" sites, slates that don't disclose who runs them, AI-generated listicles, single-source claims about a candidate's record, anything that only appears in one campaign's framing.

## By office type — what to look for and where

**Federal (House/Senate/President):** voting record via GovTrack / Congress.gov, scorecards, OpenSecrets for funding. Easiest to research — but the user is here for the hard down-ballot calls, so don't over-invest.

**State legislature & statewide (governor, AG, secretary of state, etc.):** state legislative voting records, Vote Smart, FollowTheMoney for donors, Ballotpedia profile. For incumbents, the record dominates.

**County & municipal (commissioner, council, mayor, sheriff, DA, assessor, clerk):** hardest to source online — Ballotpedia "how to find local election information," local newsroom voter guides, candidate forums (often on local LWV or library sites/YouTube), the office's own meeting records. For sheriff/DA, prioritize concrete policy (bail, prosecution priorities, cooperation with ICE, transparency) over slogans.

**School board & special districts (water, fire, hospital, community college):** lowest information density of all. Local news, candidate questionnaires, district meeting minutes. Endorsements from teachers' unions / parent groups are strong directional signals here. Expect to mark "Insufficient information" more often — and let that drive abstention honestly.

## Judicial races — deeper method

Bar-association ratings are the best starting point but **not neutral truth**. Process and caveats:

- **Read the rating AND the writeup**, not just the label. Scales vary: "Exceptionally Well Qualified / Well Qualified / Qualified / Not Qualified" or simply "Recommended / Not Recommended."
- **Cross-check ≥2 sources** — a second bar group, a court-watch project (e.g., Judge4Yourself-style local efforts), and media voter guides. Discrepancies are signal.
- **Know the institutional biases:** committees can privilege prosecutorial over defense/civil experience, and composition shapes outcomes. A "Not Qualified" can reflect inexperience, temperament complaints, *or* a non-traditional career path — read why.
- **Retention elections** (yes/no on keeping a sitting judge): look for the judge's reversal rate, any discipline, and bar-association retention recommendations. Default norm is retention absent a documented problem — but apply the voter's stated priorities (e.g., criminal-justice reform) to the judge's actual rulings where available.
- Map judicial findings onto the voter's *values* carefully: judges aren't supposed to be policy advocates, so translate "qualified + temperament + relevant rulings" rather than forcing a left/right score.

## Ballot measures / propositions — deeper method

The single biggest trap is reading the proponents' framing instead of the actual effect.

- **Find what a YES vote actually does** vs. NO (status quo). The authoritative plain-language version is the **official voter information guide / county-counsel analysis**, not the campaign for or against.
- **Read the fiscal impact statement** — who pays, how much, for how long, funded how. Many measures are fundamentally a tax/bond question dressed in mission language.
- **Read both the official arguments for and against** and note *who signed them* — the signatories reveal the real coalitions.
- **Watch the wording bias:** confusingly worded measures push voters toward "No" or non-voting. Don't let convoluted language stand in for a recommendation — translate it into plain "YES means X, NO means Y, it costs Z."
- **Money:** FollowTheMoney tracks measure committees — heavy out-of-state or single-industry funding on one side is worth flagging to the voter.
- Match to values via the *effect*: a YES that raises a sales tax to fund transit aligns with a pro-transit voter who is tax-tolerant, and conflicts for a tax-averse one — surface both dimensions when the voter's priorities pull opposite ways.

## Even-handedness checklist (run before finalizing each contest)

- Did I research independents/third-party candidates as hard as the majors?
- Did I find at least one inconvenient fact about the candidate I'm leaning toward recommending?
- Is every position labeled `[record]` or `[stated]` and sourced?
- Did I distinguish funding/endorsements (signals) from positions (substance)?
- Where info was thin, did I write "Insufficient information" instead of inferring?
